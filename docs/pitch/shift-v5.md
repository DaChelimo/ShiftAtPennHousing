# Shift@PennHousing - Stakeholder Presentation

> 23 slides. Speaker notes are short on purpose: enough to jog your memory mid-sentence,
> never enough to read from. Callbacks reference earlier ideas by name, never by slide
> number, because nobody in the room will remember "slide three" once you're ten minutes in.

## How to read the speaker notes

| Marker        | Means                                                  |
| ------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ |
| Plain bullet  | A beat to hit, in your own words                       |
| **Say:**      | Worth delivering close to verbatim                     |
| **THE LINE:** | The one sentence on that slide worth landing perfectly |
| **Callback:** | Name the earlier idea out loud (not the slide number)  |
| ⚠️            | Confirm or handle with care before you present         |

## Before you present, confirm three things

1. **The pickup cutoff hour.** You've said three hours. In the code, three hours is the
   broadcast point (still claimable then); pickup actually locks and float escalation starts
   at two hours out. Slide 16 is worded to survive either answer, but pick the number you'll
   say out loud.
2. **The Excel exit.** Today the builder exports HTML and print/PDF. There's no .csv or
   .xlsx download yet. Slide 22 is worded honestly around that. If you want to say "download
   the Excel sheet" on stage, build that export first.
3. **The persistent break card.** Written as described, folded into slide 12. Verify on a
   device that it genuinely doesn't dismiss.

No adoption numbers, worker counts, or dollar costs anywhere in this deck. No em dashes
anywhere. "The Housing Manager" stands in for a real name on slide 20.

**Audience:** a single direct superior who personally feels the daily staffing pain, not a
broad leadership audience. Lead with admin burden and worker experience, never money or
technology. Pain before fix, every slide. The floating story is the emotional centerpiece.
End with a small, low-risk ask.

## Section 5 - The app, from the manager's side

### Slide 14 - Building the schedule

**On-slide text:**

```
Problem:
Building the schedule takes forever:
- opening everyone's Excel
- constantly checking you're not slotting someone above their target hours or into a "Cannot" block.

Solution:
The app drafts a full week for the house, reading everyone's availability and hours targets (80% of the work done automatically).
For the remaining 20%, edit shifts manually while seeing each worker's availability and target hours at a glance.
```

**Visual:** the builder with the AI draft panel, and the worker picker for a single block.

**Speaker notes:**

- Setup: building a week by hand is the slowest job a student manager has, and the one most
  often done late.
- The app drafts the whole week from availability, hours targets, and staffing rules.
- Manual placement is fully supported: click a block, see who wants it, who's available, who's
  blocked, hours remaining, pre-sorted. A worker marked unavailable cannot be placed there,
  not a warning you can click past.
- **THE LINE:** "It drafts, it does not decide." Nothing goes live until a human accepts it,
  and any manager can edit anytime, for any reason.
- **Say:** "the app is very good at the work you do not want to do, and it gets out of the way
  for the work only you can do."
- **Important**: Building this in the summer showed me how different houses can be in the summer: Some closed, some open from 5.30 amall week, while others are open from 8am - 5pm on weekdays. All this can easily be configured in the app.

## Appendix (presenter prep, not slides)

### Demo plan (screenshots as backbone, live as flourish)

Script the demo as screenshots inside the deck first, so a live hiccup doesn't sink it.
Repeat a step live only if the room is warm.

1. The live schedule for a house. "One screen. Always correct." _(live schedule slide)_
2. A worker drops a shift in-app, it appears in the open feed at other houses too. "No email.
   It just happens." _(coverage between workers slide)_
3. A float assignment as a notification and an in-app card. "They cannot miss it." _(float you
   can't ignore slide)_
4. The worker taps yes, manager sees status. "No more guessing." _(same slide)_
5. The home-screen widget with next shift and float alert. _(shifts they cannot forget slide)_
6. Tap a worker to see details and the call button. _(live schedule slide)_
7. The builder: AI draft, then a manual placement showing target hours and a blocked worker.
   "It drafts. You decide." _(building the schedule slide)_
8. The hours breakdown for one worker, showing a float with house and date. "That is the
   email you no longer have to read." _(hours transparency slide)_

Safety rules: pre-load real-looking data so nothing looks empty, have phone and web already
open and logged in before the room starts, never demo live a step that only exists as a
screenshot elsewhere in the deck.

### Objections to have answers ready for (not on slides unless asked)

- **Who maintains this if you graduate or leave?** One line on handover, documentation, and
  standard supportable tools. The export in the ask slide is part of this answer.
- **Will workers actually adopt it?** It removes work for them, so adoption is a benefit. The
  pilot proves it.
- **Privacy and student data?** For scheduling: only the scheduling and contact info already
  used today, access limited by role, nothing leaves Penn's control. For the assistant, that's
  the privacy slide, and you raise it yourself.
- **Does this replace current tools overnight?** No, it runs alongside first; the ask slide is
  the exit.
- **What does it cost?** Keep it light for this room. The detailed cost case belongs in the
  next conversation, with her superiors.
- **Aren't Penn's floating rules full of exceptions?** Agreed, that's the point. The
  exceptions (Harnwell training required, never below one worker, Harnwell never a float
  destination) are built in as hard rules the system enforces.
- **Can the assistant give a wrong answer?** Any assistant can, which is why it's a first stop,
  never a replacement for paging. It answers from the desk's own documents, which keeps it
  grounded. If pressed: the current failure mode is a new worker guessing with zero
  information, and this is strictly better than that.
- **Who decides what goes into the assistant?** Housing decides. It only knows what it's
  given.
- **What if a house games the automatic float chain?** The system reads actual staffing
  instead of asking a house to self-report. That's the point of the three-paths slide.
