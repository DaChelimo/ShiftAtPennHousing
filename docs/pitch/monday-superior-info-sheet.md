## 1. ONE-LINE PITCH (the "what is this")

Shift@PennHousing is a single app for Penn Housing desk staffing that replaces the scattered
emails, spreadsheets, group texts, and phone calls used today with one live, always-correct
schedule. It automates shift coverage (drops, pickups, swaps, and floats) and reminds workers
so shifts do not get forgotten, so managers stop drowning in email and desks stop going empty.

Scope for context (do not dwell): 13 Penn college houses, one shared live schedule, roles for
Student Workers, Student Managers, RSMs, and Housing Managers.

## 3. THE PROBLEM (status quo). Tell it as her day, not as a list

### 3A. Problem: Email overload

- Today, every swap, pickup, and drop generates emails she has to read, track, and reconcile.
- There is no single place where the current truth lives. Her inbox IS the system, and it is
  noisy, easy to miss, and never up to date.
- Slide idea: a cluttered inbox visual. Caption: "This is the scheduling system today."

### 3B. Problem: Manual documentation just to approve hours

- When a worker works a desk that is not their home desk (a walk or a float), someone must
  email the RSM and the student managers of BOTH the home desk and the covering desk, purely
  to create a paper trail so the worker's hours can be approved.
- This is manual, repetitive, and error-prone. If the email is missed, hours approval stalls.

### 3C. Problem: No live source of truth

- Because the truth is spread across inboxes, texts, and spreadsheets, there is no one place
  that shows, right now, who is actually working each desk and who covered for whom.
- She cannot glance at one screen and trust it.

### 3D. Problem: No-shows from forgotten shifts

- A worker picks up a shift, then forgets about it. The desk sits empty.
- This is one of her most critical pains. It is worse in the fall semester.
- The root cause is simple and human: a pickup lives in a text or an email, not somewhere the
  worker will actually see when the day comes.

### 3E. Problem: Floating. THE CENTERPIECE STORY

Set the scene simply first: "Floating is when a worker from one house is sent to cover a
different house that is short-staffed." There are two kinds.

- Kind 1, mid-shift float: someone at another house does not show up at, say, 8pm. A worker
  from Harnwell is sent over. This one is manageable today because it is a quick phone call.
- Kind 2, planned float (a week ahead): this is where everything breaks. Walk through it as a
  chain, because each link fails in a way she will recognize.

The chain today:
1. A worker drops a shift a week out and cannot find coverage (in fall it is hard to get
   someone from another house unless you personally know them).
2. The student manager sees no one is picking it up and sends a coverage request.
3. The Harnwell RSM reaches out to a worker on the Harnwell desk and tells them to float.
4. Floating is a two-way agreement: the worker must reply yes or no, AND add it to their
   calendar.

Now the FIVE ways it fails today (this is the money moment of the story, present each as a
tiny "what goes wrong" beat):

- FAILURE 1. The worker never sees the email, so they never even know they were supposed to
  float.
- FAILURE 2. The worker forgets to update their calendar, oversleeps or autopilots to their
  home desk, and is in the wrong place. The desk they were meant to cover is empty.
- FAILURE 3. The worker adds it to their calendar but never replies yes or no, so the RSM or
  HMOD has no idea whether anyone is actually coming.
- FAILURE 4. Even when everything goes right, the destination desk has no way to reach the
  floater who is on the way. Example: a floater is walking from Quad to Hill. The person at
  Hill waits five minutes, the floater has not arrived, so they call Harnwell (instead of
  Quad). Harnwell pages HMOD. HMOD secures paid Allied coverage. Two minutes later the floater
  arrives. Now the desk is double-covered AND the school paid Allied for nothing.
- FAILURE 5. The float protocol can be gamed. Example: Quad has 3 workers, Harnwell has 2,
  Hill needs a floater. Quad should send someone, but Quad simply says "we have no floater."
  There is no way to check, so Harnwell is forced to float instead. The rules are not
  enforceable, so people can quietly opt out and push the burden onto others.

Land the story: "Every one of these is a person doing their best inside a process that gives
them no reminders, no live truth, no way to reach each other, and no way to enforce fairness."

## 4. HOW IT IS DONE TODAY (the tools, and why each one hurts)

Present as a simple 3-column idea: Tool today, What it is used for, Why it fails.

- Spreadsheets and Excel: building and sharing the schedule. Fails because it goes stale the
  moment anything changes, and it does not remind anyone of anything.
- Group texts and GroupMe: last-minute coverage and swaps. Fails because messages get buried,
  it is first-come-first-served chaos, and there is no record.
- Email: swap and drop requests, and documentation for hours approval. Fails because it is
  easy to miss, slow, and it is not a live picture of anything.
- Phone calls: confirming who is coming, chasing floaters. Fails because you can only reach
  someone if you happen to have their number and they happen to answer.

The through-line: none of these tools talk to each other, none of them is live, and none of
them reminds anyone. The manager becomes the human glue holding it all together.

## 5. OUR SOLUTION. Feature mapped to the exact pain it kills

Present each as: "Remember [pain]? Here is what happens now." Keep the language plain.

### 5A. Kills email overload: everything happens in the app, not the inbox

- Swaps, pickups, drops, and floats all happen inside the app between the people involved.
- The manager is no longer cc'd on a stream of requests. The app is the system now, not her
  inbox.
- Swaps and handoffs are agreed directly between the two workers with a tap to accept. No
  manager approval step, no manager email.

### 5B. Kills documentation burden: the live schedule IS the record

- The schedule is the single source of truth. When anyone drops, picks up, swaps, or floats,
  it updates instantly for everyone.
- Cross-house coverage is shown right on the schedule with clear color coding (a worker
  covering another house's desk is visibly marked, with their home house shown).
- Each worker's hours report automatically breaks their week into hours worked at home, hours
  worked while floated out, and hours worked from picking up another house's shift. That
  breakdown is the documentation. No emails to the RSM and two student managers just to get
  hours approved.

### 5C. Kills "no live picture": one screen, always correct

- Anyone with permission can open the schedule and see, right now, who is on every desk.
- They can also scroll back to see who worked any past block. The calendar is the record.

### 5D. Kills no-shows: the shift lives where the worker will see it

- Home-screen widgets. A worker's next shift, and any float they owe, sits on their phone home
  screen. They do not need to remember to check a calendar, because it is already in front of
  them. This is the direct answer to "they picked it up and forgot."
- A second widget shows open shifts they could pick up, both at their own house and at other
  houses, at a glance.
- Personal notifications for your own shift and your own float are mandatory and cannot be
  silenced. If it affects you, you get told.

### 5E. Kills the floating failures, one by one (put this next to the Section 3E chain)

- Answers FAILURE 1 (never saw the email): the float arrives as a push notification and an
  in-app card. No buried email.
- Answers FAILURE 2 (forgot, went to wrong desk): the float is automatically on their schedule
  and on their home-screen widget. Nothing to manually add.
- Answers FAILURE 3 (never replied): the worker acknowledges yes or no inside the app, and the
  system sends escalating reminders before the deadline (at 6 hours, 2 hours, 1 hour, 30
  minutes, and 5 minutes before). Managers can see the acknowledgment status at a glance. No
  more guessing whether someone is coming.
- Answers FAILURE 4 (could not reach the floater en route): tap the floater on the schedule to
  see their details and a "Call [name]" button that opens the phone dialer ready to call. This
  works today, including for an incoming floater at the desk you are covering. Combined with the
  system knowing coverage is already assigned, this prevents the panic-page to Allied while the
  floater is two minutes away.
- Answers FAILURE 5 (Quad lies about having a floater): the rules are enforced by the system,
  not by trust. The system itself decides who floats based on real staffing. A house cannot be
  left below one worker, and the system will not let a house dodge its turn by claiming it has
  no one. The protocol is enforced in software, so it cannot be quietly gamed.

### 5F. Bonus: coverage is found automatically, in the right order, before it becomes a crisis

- When a desk will actually be empty, the system automatically works through the coverage
  options in order: notify subscribed workers, offer it for pickup (including at other eligible
  houses), then float, and only then fall back to paid Allied coverage.
- It only does this when a desk would truly be empty, so it never spams people or wastes paid
  coverage on a desk that still has someone on it.
- (Light touch only for Monday: this is also how the app protects budget, because paid Allied
  becomes the rare last resort instead of the default. Save the money argument for the higher
  up pitch.)

## 6. WHAT WE HAVE BUILT (plain-language capability list)

Use this as a single "This is real, and it works today" slide. Keep it to short phrases.

Built and working:
- One live schedule shared across all 13 houses, correct in real time.
- In-app drops, pickups, and claims (including picking up shifts at other eligible houses).
- In-app swaps (one-time, float, and permanent) and one-way handoffs, agreed peer to peer.
- Automatic float assignment with in-app yes/no acknowledgment and escalating reminders.
- Automatic coverage cascade (notify, offer for pickup, float, then Allied as last resort).
- Home-screen widgets: your next shift with float alerts, and open shifts you can grab.
- Push notifications for anything that affects you personally.
- Automatic hours breakdown (home, floated, cross-house pickup) for easy approval.
- Tap any shift (including an incoming floater) to see the worker's contact details and a
  one-tap "Call [name]" button that opens the phone dialer, for reaching a floater en route.
- Works on both iPhone and Android, plus a web view for managers.

Newer capability worth a brief wow, if time allows (do not lead with it):
- AI-assisted schedule building that can generate a full draft schedule for a house, which the
  manager then reviews and accepts. Frame it simply: "It can draft the schedule for you, and
  you stay in control."

## 10. RISKS AND OBJECTIONS TO PRE-EMPT (pitch-expert layer)

Prepare a quick answer for each. Do not put these on slides unless asked. keep them in the
back pocket.

- "Who maintains this and what happens if you graduate or leave?" This is the most likely
  hard question from a university. Have a one-line answer ready about handover, documentation,
  and it being built on standard, supportable tools.
- "Will workers actually adopt it?" Answer: it removes work for them (no chasing, no
  forgetting, shifts on their home screen), so adoption is a benefit, not a burden. The pilot
  proves it.
- "What about privacy and student data?" Have a calm, simple answer: it only uses the
  scheduling and contact info already used today, access is limited by role, and nothing
  leaves Penn's control. Do not over-claim; if unsure of a detail, say you will confirm.
- "Does this replace our staff or our current tools overnight?" Answer: no. It runs alongside
  first. The pilot is reversible.
- "What does it cost?" For Monday, keep it light: the pitch is about her daily pain, not
  budget. If pressed, say the detailed cost and savings case is exactly what you want to bring
  to the next conversation with her superiors.
- "The floating rules at Penn are complicated and have exceptions." Answer: agreed, and that is
  the point. The exceptions (Harnwell training, never leaving a desk below one worker, Harnwell
  never being a float destination) are already built in as hard rules the system enforces.
- Demo failure: mitigated by the screenshots-first plan in Section 9.
- Over-teching the room: mitigated by the translation table in Section 8. Watch for any slide
  that uses an internal term.
